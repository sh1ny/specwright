# Ideation techniques

Use this when the user has no direction yet, or a part of the design is stuck. The aim is a decision, not a pile of ideas. Record the outcome, not the session.

## Running a session

1. **Open wide.** Invite the user to describe the game in their head. Ask "anything more?" once. Listen for a verb, a feeling and a constraint.
2. **Find the gap.** Is there no hook, no verb, a flat loop, no reason to return, or nothing that feels fresh? Use the symptom map below.
3. **Pick one to three techniques** for that gap. Do not run them all. Pull one at a time.
4. **Generate with the user.** You supply sparks and the user reacts. Defer judgement while ideas flow; do not evaluate in the same breath.
5. **Switch angle** when ideas cluster around one shape. Change the technique, not the volume.
6. **Converge** to two or three named directions. For each write: the loop in one line, the fantasy, the hook, and the biggest risk.
7. **The user chooses.** Do not choose for them. Ask whether they would start this game today.
8. **Name what to prototype first**: the riskiest assumption of the chosen direction.
9. **Write down** the chosen direction in `brief.md` or the GDD, plus one decision-log line. Ideas the user did not endorse go nowhere in the documents.

When the direction is still uncertain after this, offer two or three small competing prototypes instead of forcing a choice on paper.

## Symptom to technique

| Symptom | Try |
|---|---|
| "I have nothing yet" | verb list; analogy from another medium; random-input cards |
| "It feels generic" | genre-rule inversion; constraint cards; SCAMPER |
| "The loop is flat" | loops and arcs; MDA read backwards; reverse brainstorming |
| "Progression is just numbers" | player-motivation check; MDA read backwards; morphological box |
| "I cannot pick between ideas" | paper prototype; Six Thinking Hats; five whys |
| "Everything looks like the usual one" | worst-possible-idea; analogy; morphological box |
| "It is fun for a minute, then not" | flow channel; loops and arcs; player-motivation check |
| "I do not know why this exists" | five whys; lenses |

## Techniques, in the order of the gap they address

### Finding a starting point

**Verb list.** A common way to start in small-team design: list the verbs the player does, then choose a few that carry the game.
- Which three verbs could the player do for an hour without boredom?
- What is the verb nobody else would pick for this setting?
- Which verb pairs make a decision when they conflict (hide or run)?

**Analogy from another medium or a real system.** Borrow the shape of something that is not a game: a market, a tide, a kitchen rush, a library.
- What is the thing you want people to feel, and where else do they feel it?
- What would the "rules" of that system be, if the player had to run it?
- Which part of the analogy would be tedious in a game, and what replaces it?

**Random-input or constraint cards.** Draw an odd prompt or impose a limit, in the spirit of Eno and Schmidt's Oblique Strategies. Give yourself one input verb, one resource and one place, picked at random.
- What if the game had only one button?
- What if the player could not see their own character?
- What if one resource were shared with the enemy?

### Making an idea less ordinary

**Genre-rule inversion.** Take one rule the genre treats as given and flip it.
- What if the player must lose to progress?
- What if the safest place is the middle of the danger?
- What would the opposite of this genre's win condition be?

**SCAMPER.** Osborn's checklist, set out by Eberle: substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate, reverse. Run a single mechanic through it.
- What can be removed without the loop collapsing?
- What can be combined with the existing rule?
- What does the mechanic do if used for a different purpose?

**Morphological box.** Zwicky's grid: list the dimensions of the game (verb, setting, resource, opponent, win condition), list three options in each, and combine across columns.
- What odd combination does a random path through the grid give?
- Which dimension is fixed because the user loves it, and which can vary?
- Which cell made the user laugh or object?

### Reading an idea as a design

**MDA, read in both directions.** Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek: mechanics produce dynamics, dynamics produce aesthetics (the felt experience). Read forwards to predict, backwards to design: start from the feeling and ask what must happen to produce it.
- Which feeling do we want the player to have at the busiest moment?
- Which dynamics make that feeling (pressure, trade, surprise)?
- Which mechanic produces that dynamic, and is there a simpler one?

**Lenses.** Schell's collection of questions about a game. Pick only a few that fit the gap.
- What does the player actually do, moment to moment?
- What problem does the player solve, and is it the one you meant?
- What would a first-time player and an expert each say it is about?

**Loops and arcs.** Cook's model of how skills are learned: a loop repeats and teaches a skill, an arc is consumed once. A flat loop often lacks a growing skill, or has arcs standing in for loops.
- What does the player get better at, and how do they know?
- Which parts will be spent once and run out?
- What does the player learn on the tenth pass that they did not on the first?

### Stress-testing

**Reverse brainstorming.** Ask how to make the game boring, unfair or confusing, then invert each answer.
- How would we make the player want to quit in two minutes?
- Which of those already exists in the current plan?

**Worst-possible-idea.** Produce deliberately terrible versions and find the grain of something real in them.
- What is the worst way to handle death? What would be the least bad opposite?

**Six Thinking Hats.** De Bono's method of taking one stance at a time: facts, feelings, caution, benefits, new ideas, process. Use it to review a direction without a debate.
- Facts only: what do we know about this idea?
- Caution only: what would go wrong?
- Benefits only: why might it work?

**Five whys.** Ask why of a fantasy, five times, until the real feeling shows.
- Why do you want the player to feel hunted? Why does that matter?

### Checking motivation and pace

**Player-motivation check.** Start from self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan): autonomy, competence, relatedness. Ask which of the three the game serves and how. Bartle's types are vocabulary for a conversation, never a measurement and never a split of real players. Note that expected rewards can crowd out enjoyment of the act, while signals of mastery tend not to.
- Where does the player choose, and does the choice matter?
- Where does the player feel better at something than before?
- Where do they feel connected to someone or something?

**Flow channel.** Csikszentmihalyi's model: challenge rising with skill stays in the channel, too much is anxiety, too little is boredom. Use it for pacing ideas: a sawtooth of rising tension and release.
- Where is the first rest, and where is the first peak?
- What lowers the challenge when the player is lost?
- What raises it when they are bored?

### Making it concrete

**Paper or toy prototype.** Play a version with cards, dice or a spreadsheet before building.
- Can the core decision be played in ten minutes with paper?
- What happened that the design did not predict?
- Did anyone ask to play again?

## Closing a session

Write down, in order: the chosen direction, why it was chosen, the riskiest assumption, and what to build first to test it. If the user has been through several pivots, ask one plain question: would you start this game today?
